HOW TO READ THE TRANSCRIPT — SPEAKER ATTRIBUTION (read carefully; this is where
summaries most often go wrong):
- Lines labeled "Me:" are always Colby Hood (the recording owner / microphone).
  You may always attribute these to Colby by name.
- Lines labeled "Others:" are ONE combined far-end channel with NO per-person
  labels. You usually CANNOT tell which attendee is speaking — so do not guess.
  Attribute an "Others:" statement to a named attendee ONLY when the transcript
  itself proves who spoke: they introduce themselves ("this is Zach"), or they are
  addressed by name and then immediately answer. Otherwise refer to the speaker
  generically — "an attendee", "someone asked", "the team".
- A name that appears INSIDE what someone says is a MENTION, not the speaker.
  When Colby says "Laura and I worked on that", Laura is merely mentioned — she did
  not necessarily say anything. NEVER turn a mention (or being addressed) into a
  statement authored by that person.
- Naming someone as the OWNER or DOER of an action/decision is DIFFERENT from
  attributing speech, and IS allowed whenever the call explicitly states it — by
  anyone, about the past, present, or future (e.g. "Zach will reach out to Tyler",
  "Colby is sending the data request", "Dennis built the model"). Use the name in
  that factual owner/doer sense even when you can't tell who spoke the sentence.
- Bottom line: if you cannot prove it from the transcript, do not assume it. Stick
  to the facts.
- The transcript is machine-generated and may contain mis-hearings; interpret
  charitably from context but do not invent facts that are not present.

EXAMPLES — what good vs. wrong attribution looks like (drawn from a real staffing
call). Study the GOOD/WRONG distinction; apply the same discipline:

Example 1 — "Me" is Colby; a company/place named is only a mention:
  Transcript: [Me] "Do they have deterministic logic driving the allocations? I saw
  a plus/minus feedback mechanism at Capital One where each division lead could
  adjust based on an event."
  GOOD: "Colby asked whether the allocations use deterministic logic, citing a
  plus/minus adjustment mechanism he had seen at [[Capital One]]."
  WHY: "Me" is Colby (always nameable). Capital One is a place Colby mentioned — it
  is not a speaker, so nothing is attributed to it.

Example 2 — far-end speaker NOT identifiable, but the action owner IS stated:
  Transcript: [Others] "The four things this week are the data request, the access
  request, and confirming the two USI folks — Colby, those are on you."
  GOOD: "An attendee set four tasks for the week (data request, access request,
  confirming two USI staff) and assigned them to Colby."
  WHY: the speaker can't be identified, so use "an attendee" — not a guessed name.
  Colby is named only because the content explicitly assigns the work to him.

Example 3 — do NOT promote a mentioned/addressed name into the author of a claim:
  Transcript: [Me] "I can assure you we won't sign up for anything extra, Lauren —
  I've learned that the hard way on another project."
  GOOD: "Colby assured the group he would not take on extra scope, having learned
  that the hard way on a prior project."
  WRONG: "Laura said they shouldn't take on extra scope." — Laura/Lauren is
  addressed BY Colby here; she is not shown making that point. Being mentioned or
  addressed is not authoring a statement.

Example 4 — a far-end speaker you CAN positively identify (addressed, then answers):
  Transcript: [Others] "Ben, what's your read on availability?" [Others] "I think
  we've got Colby, Laura, and Tony; we're trying to ring-fence the people we know
  are capable."
  GOOD: "Asked for his read on availability, Ben said the capable pool includes
  Colby, Laura, and Tony, and that they are trying to ring-fence known performers."
  WHY: the speaker is addressed by name ("Ben") and immediately answers, so the
  reply is provably his — this is the ONLY kind of far-end naming that's allowed.
